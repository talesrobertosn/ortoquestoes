PROXIMAL HUMERAL FRACTURES
Significant controversy and variability surrounds the treatment
of proximal humeral fractures. The large heterogeneity of injury patterns, patient factors, and surgical treatment options
make this fracture entity challenging to study and approach
algorithmically. No single treatment protocol or algorithm has
been proved to be universally effective. Nonoperative treatment for two-, three-, and four-part proximal humeral fractures has been shown to be of benefit in elderly patients, but a
high percentage report pain and loss of function. With operative treatment, radiographic results may be superior but functional results are not better. The Proximal Fracture of the Humerus: Evaluation by Randomization (ProHFER) trial and
5-year follow-up study, as well as other studies, have been unable to demonstrate superiority of operative over nonoperative
treatment. To date, there remains a high degree of variation in
the management of these fractures regionally, particularly
within health care systems focused on value-based care.

humeral fractures is that of Neer (Fig. 62.17). Classification

the humeral head, the lesser and greater tuberosities, and the
proximal humeral shaft. The criterion for displacement is more
than 1 cm of separation of a part or angulation of 45 degrees.

translations greater than 100% (no bony contact) comprise
10% of proximal humeral fracture patterns and need to be

RADIOGRAPHIC EVALUATION
An anteroposterior view of the shoulder in the plane of the
scapula, a lateral view of the scapula (Y view) (Fig. 62.19),

NONOPERATIVE TREATMENT
The range of motion of the shoulder joint accommodates
moderate angular deformity, typically without significant
functional loss. An elderly, infirm patient can tolerate

functional loss better than a young, active patient. One
must assess if fracture displacement, angulation, and stability are appropriate for a given patient’s physiologic age. If
nonoperative management is chosen, a sling is used for
comfort, and a physical therapy regimen with pendulum
exercises is started, usually within 1 to 2 weeks. Generally,
the longer the period of immobilization and therapy, the
greater the disability. Early (,72 hours) passive mobilization has been found to be safe and restore function more
effectively than conventional immobilization (3 weeks) followed by physical therapy.

COMPLICATIONS
The most common complication of proximal humeral fractures is loss of motion (stiffness). Early physical therapy is
associated with improved motion, but many patients do not
recover full motion even with early physical therapy. Screw
cut-out and humeral head collapse continues to drive the
high rate of reoperations, particularly in the elderly population, despite advances in plating technology. Malunion can
result from unstable or delayed fracture fixation, patient factors, and poor surgical technique. In older patients with
limited functional demands, malunion generally is well tolerated, but it may be debilitating in younger patients because of
poor shoulder function, impingement, or rotator cuff pathology. Osteonecrosis is relatively uncommon and its presence
does not always result in a poor outcome. It may be evident
radiographically but cause minimal symptoms, particularly
in elderly patients with lower functional demand.

FRACTURES OF THE HUMERAL
SHAFT
Fractures of the humeral shaft account for approximately 3%
of all fractures; many may be successfully treated nonoperatively. The range of motion afforded by the shoulder and elbow joints, coupled with a tolerance for small amounts of
shortening, allow radiographic imperfections that cause minimal functional deficit and are well tolerated by the patient. A
mobile humeral shaft fracture at 6-weeks’ follow-up has been
found to be a predictor of nonunion (82% sensitivity and 99%

specificity). Fracture mobility at this time point is 6.5 times
more likely to proceed to nonunion at 6 months; patients
should be carefully counseled about converting to ORIF. A
modified radiographic union score has been described as a
reliable way to predict nonunion (Rivero et al). It was adapted
for use from the tibia and consists of four states of the humeral cortex:
1. No callus present
2. Some callus present (not bridging the fracture)
3. Callus bridging the fracture
4. Continuity of the cortex
